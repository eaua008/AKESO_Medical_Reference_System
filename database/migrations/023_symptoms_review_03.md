## Coughing up blood (hemoptysis)  (`sym_hemoptysis`)

**Added to the description:** In adults the commonest causes are bronchitis, bronchiectasis, necrotizing pneumonia, tuberculosis and COPD, with lung cancer important in heavy smokers; in children, lower respiratory infection and inhaled foreign bodies predominate. In up to half of patients no cause is found, and most of these settle within 6 months.

**New causes:**
- COPD flare
- Necrotizing pneumonia or lung abscess
- Inhaled foreign body (children)
- Mitral stenosis

**New red flags:**
- Any hemoptysis in a heavy smoker
- Absent breath sounds on one side or breathlessness at rest

**New references:**

## Craving ice, soil or other non-food items (pica)  (`sym_pica`)

**Added to the description:** It is diagnosed when the eating of non-food material lasts at least a month and is not normal for the person's age or culture (it is not diagnosed under age 2). Clay can bind iron and zinc and worsen the deficiency, and pica often coexists with autism or intellectual disability.

**New causes:**
- Intellectual disability

**New red flags:**
- Swallowing hair, sharp objects or other hard items

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Pica: Attia E, Walsh BT. Pica. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Aug 2025. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/feeding-and-eating-disorders/pica

## Dark urine  (`sym_dark_urine`)

**Added to the description:** Conjugated bilirubin can darken the urine before jaundice is visible, so dark urine is an early sign of hepatitis or bile duct blockage; unconjugated bilirubin (as in hemolysis) does not pass into urine. Several drugs, including rifampicin, phenazopyridine, levodopa, methyldopa and senna, change urine color.

**New causes:**
- Bile duct blockage (gallstones, tumor)
- Leptospirosis (jaundice and kidney involvement)
- Specific drugs: rifampicin, phenazopyridine, levodopa, methyldopa, senna

**New red flags:**
- Abdominal pain with fever and jaundice (possible cholangitis)
- Bruising, small red spots or bleeding

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Jaundice: Tholey D. Jaundice. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Aug 2025. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/hepatic-and-biliary-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-liver-disease/jaundice

## Decreased urine output (oliguria)  (`sym_decreased_urine`)

**Added to the description:** Some references use under 500 mL in 24 hours for adults, and under 1 mL/kg/h in newborns. A palpable bladder suggests outlet obstruction, a sudden drop in a catheterised patient with normal blood pressure suggests a blocked catheter, and dark brown urine suggests myoglobin from muscle breakdown.

**New causes:**
- Kidney stones or enlarged prostate
- Muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis)
- Severe dengue with plasma leakage

**New red flags:**
- Full, palpable bladder with inability to pass urine
- Dark brown urine with muscle pain (possible rhabdomyolysis)

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Oliguria: Berry C. Oliguria. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Oct 2025. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/critical-care-medicine/approach-to-the-critically-ill-patient/oliguria

## Diarrhea  (`sym_diarrhea`)

**Added to the description:** It arises from too much osmotic load, increased secretion or reduced absorption, or fast transit, and infection is the commonest cause of acute secretory diarrhea. Diarrhea that wakes the patient at night, comes with weight loss or lasts weeks points to an organic cause rather than irritable bowel syndrome.

**New causes:**
- Celiac disease and lactose intolerance
- Overactive thyroid
- Laxative misuse
- Clostridioides difficile after antibiotics

**New red flags:**
- Diarrhea that wakes the patient at night, or with weight loss
- Recent antibiotics or hospital stay (possible C. difficile)

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Diarrhea: Gotfried J. Diarrhea. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Apr 2026. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/symptoms-of-gastrointestinal-disorders/diarrhea

## Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)  (`sym_dysphagia`)

**Added to the description:** Oropharyngeal dysphagia usually comes from nerve or muscle disease such as stroke, Parkinson disease or myasthenia gravis; esophageal dysphagia comes from motility disorders or mechanical narrowing. Upper endoscopy is the usual first test, with biopsies for eosinophilic esophagitis if no structural cause is seen.

**New causes:**
- Parkinson disease or myasthenia gravis
- Rabies (painful throat spasms)

**New red flags:**
- Repeated chest infections from aspiration

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Dysphagia: Lynch KL. Dysphagia. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Feb 2026. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/esophageal-and-swallowing-disorders/dysphagia

## Dizziness / lightheadedness  (`sym_dizziness`)

**Added to the description:** Common inner-ear causes are benign positional vertigo, Meniere disease and vestibular neuritis or labyrinthitis; the commonest central cause is vestibular migraine, followed by drug effects, especially blood pressure medicines. Ringing, fullness or hearing loss in the ear suggests an inner-ear cause, whereas nystagmus that is not suppressed by fixing the gaze suggests a central cause.

**New causes:**
- Meniere disease
- Vestibular migraine
- Labyrinthitis
- Severe dengue with low blood pressure

**New red flags:**
- Unsteady walking or inability to stand
- Constant severe vertigo lasting more than an hour

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Dizziness and Vertigo: Formeister EJ. Dizziness and Vertigo. Merck Manual Professional Version. ## Epigastric pain  (`sym_epigastric_pain`)

**Added to the description:** Medicines such as NSAIDs, opioids and iron, delayed stomach emptying and celiac disease can also cause it, and when all tests are normal the diagnosis is functional dyspepsia. If ischemia is possible, cardiac causes are checked first; endoscopy is advised for new symptoms after about age 60.

**New causes:**
- Medicines (NSAIDs, opioids, iron)
- Stomach or esophageal cancer
- Myocardial ischemia
- Early appendicitis

**New red flags:**
- New symptoms after age 60
- Symptoms not improving on acid-suppressing medicine

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Dyspepsia: Gotfried J. Dyspepsia. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Apr 2026. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/gastrointestinal-disorders/symptoms-of-gastrointestinal-disorders/dyspepsia

## Excessive salivation / drooling  (`sym_hypersalivation`)

**Added to the description:** In children, drooling without a barking cough strongly suggests epiglottitis rather than croup, and drooling with stridor means the airway may need urgent securing; the child should not be upset or examined in the throat until then. Excess saliva with tears, sweating, small pupils and muscle twitching suggests organophosphate poisoning.

**New causes:**
- Epiglottitis

**New red flags:**
- Small pupils, sweating, vomiting and muscle twitching (possible organophosphate poisoning)

**New references:**

## Excessive thirst (polydipsia)  (`sym_polydipsia`)

**Added to the description:** Diabetes mellitus is the commonest cause of thirst with heavy urination; others are diuretics, diabetes insipidus and primary polydipsia. People with primary polydipsia may drink up to 6 L a day and usually do not wake at night to pass urine; drinking that much can cause dangerously low sodium.

**New causes:**
- Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (vasopressin resistance)

**New red flags:**
- Headache, vomiting or confusion after drinking very large amounts (possible low sodium)

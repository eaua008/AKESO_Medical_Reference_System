## Abdominal pain  (`sym_abdominal_pain`)

**Added to the description:** Visceral pain is dull and poorly localised (epigastric for the stomach and duodenum, periumbilical for the small bowel and appendix, suprapubic for the colon), whereas pain from an inflamed parietal peritoneum is sharp and well localised; appendicitis classically starts periumbilically and migrates to the right lower quadrant. Causes outside the abdomen, such as myocardial infarction, lower-lobe pneumonia and diabetic ketoacidosis, must not be missed.

**New causes:**
- Intestinal obstruction
- Acute pancreatitis
- Ectopic pregnancy and other gynecologic causes (ovarian torsion, pelvic inflammatory disease)
- Mesenteric ischemia or abdominal aortic aneurysm (older adults)
- Acute hepatitis
- Causes outside the abdomen: myocardial infarction, lower-lobe pneumonia, diabetic ketoacidosis

**New red flags:**
- Pain out of proportion to examination findings in an older adult (possible mesenteric ischemia)
- Missed period or positive pregnancy test with pain, bleeding or fainting (possible ectopic pregnancy)

## Abdominal swelling (distension or ascites)  (`sym_abdominal_swelling`)

**Added to the description:** The classic causes of a swollen abdomen are remembered as fluid, flatus, fat, fetus and feces. When ascites is tapped, a serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) of 1.1 g/dL or more points to portal hypertension (cirrhosis, heart failure), while a lower gradient suggests peritoneal causes such as tuberculosis or malignancy.

**New causes:**
- Peritoneal tuberculosis
- Constipation and fecal loading
- Pancreatic ascites after pancreatitis

**New red flags:**
- Rapid increase in abdominal girth or new ascites in a patient with known liver disease

**New references:**
- StatPearls - Ascites: Goosenberg E, Kudaravalli P, Samant H. Ascites. [Updated 2025 Nov 30]. In: StatPearls [Internet]. Treasure Island (FL): StatPearls Publishing. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470482/

**New disease links:**

## Acid regurgitation (sour fluid or food coming back up)  (`sym_regurgitation`)

**Added to the description:** Unlike vomiting, it comes without nausea or forceful abdominal contraction. Regurgitation responds less well to acid-suppressing medicines than heartburn does, so persistent regurgitation on a proton pump inhibitor is a reason for further testing. In infants, effortless spitting up after feeds is common and usually physiological.

**New causes:**
- Physiological reflux in infants
- Esophageal stricture or Zenker's diverticulum (undigested food, bad breath)

**New red flags:**
- Anemia or persistent vomiting
- Poor weight gain, forceful vomiting or feeding refusal in an infant

**New references:**

## Agitation, anxiety or hallucinations  (`sym_agitation`)

**Added to the description:** Delirium is suggested when agitation begins suddenly, fluctuates through the day and comes with poor attention; its quiet, withdrawn (hypoactive) form is easily missed. In furious rabies, episodes of agitation, hallucinations and hydrophobia alternate with lucid intervals.

**New causes:**
- Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia)
- Head injury or stroke
- Bacterial meningitis
- Liver failure (hepatic encephalopathy)
- Urinary retention, constipation or untreated pain in older adults

**New red flags:**
- Sudden onset in an older adult (possible delirium)
- Headache, neck stiffness and fever (possible meningitis)

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Delirium: Huang J. Delirium. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Feb 2025. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/neurologic-disorders/delirium-and-dementia/delirium

## Black, tarry stools (melena)  (`sym_melena`)

**Added to the description:** About 100 to 200 mL of blood in the upper gastrointestinal tract is needed to turn the stool black and tarry, and melena can continue for several days after the bleeding has stopped. Patients are risk-stratified early (for example with the Glasgow-Blatchford score) to decide on admission and endoscopy.

**New causes:**
- Gastrointestinal bleeding in severe dengue
- Intestinal bleeding in typhoid fever (usually in the third week)
- Bleeding from the small bowel or right colon (less common)

**New red flags:**
- Jaundice, ascites or known liver disease (possible variceal bleeding)
- Black stools during dengue, especially around the time the fever settles

## Blister-like rash (vesicles)  (`sym_vesicular_rash`)

**Added to the description:** In unvaccinated people, varicella lesions progress quickly from macules to papules to vesicles before crusting; breakthrough varicella after vaccination is milder, usually with fewer than 50 lesions that are mostly papules. In scabies, small vesicles may appear at the ends of burrows, especially on the hands and wrists.

**New causes:**
- Scabies (vesicles at the ends of burrows)
- Insect bites (papular urticaria)

**New red flags:**
- Vesicles on the tip of the nose or around the eye (possible ophthalmic zoster)

**New references:**
- CDC - Clinical Overview of Chickenpox (Varicella): Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Clinical Overview of Chickenpox (Varicella). Atlanta (GA): CDC; reviewed 2024 Jul 15. https://www.cdc.gov/chickenpox/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html

## Blood in stool (hematochezia)  (`sym_bloody_stool`)

**Added to the description:** Streaks of bright blood on the toilet paper or on the surface of formed stool point to an anorectal source, whereas blood mixed into the stool or maroon stools suggest a source higher in the colon. In the tropics, bloody diarrhea (dysentery) is most often due to Shigella or Entamoeba histolytica, and intestinal bleeding is also a complication of typhoid fever and severe dengue.

**New causes:**
- Typhoid fever (intestinal hemorrhage)
- Severe dengue with bleeding
- Meckel's diverticulum (children)
- Intussusception in infants (red currant jelly stools)

**New red flags:**
- Bloody diarrhea in a child who then passes little urine or becomes pale (possible hemolytic uremic syndrome)
- Bleeding during dengue or with a low platelet count

**New references:**
- ACG Guideline - Acute Lower GI Bleeding (2023): Sengupta N, Feuerstein JD, Jairath V, et al. Management of Patients With Acute Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding: An Updated ACG Guideline. Am J Gastroenterol. 2023;118(2):208-231. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36735555/

## Blood in urine (hematuria / cola-colored urine)  (`sym_hematuria`)

**Added to the description:** Red or brown urine is not always blood: beets, rifampicin, myoglobin and free hemoglobin can look the same, so a dipstick and urine microscopy are needed. Microscopic hematuria is 3 or more red blood cells per high-power field on a properly collected sample; dysmorphic red cells, red cell casts and protein in the urine favor a glomerular source.

**New causes:**
- Leptospirosis (kidney involvement)
- Dengue with bleeding
- Enlarged prostate
- Polycystic kidney disease
- Sickle cell disease or trait
- Injury to the kidney or urinary tract
- Muscle breakdown or hemolysis (dipstick positive without red cells)

**New red flags:**
- Passing blood clots or being unable to pass urine
- Blood in the urine after an injury

**New references:**
- AUA/SUFU Guideline - Microhematuria: American Urological Association / Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine & Urogenital Reconstruction. Microhematuria: AUA/SUFU Guideline (2020; amended 2025). https://www.auanet.org/guidelines-and-quality/guidelines/microhematuria

**New disease links:**

- leptospirosis (2/10, x0.5)
- dengue (2/10, x0.4)

## Blurred vision  (`sym_blurred_vision`)

**Added to the description:** Asking whether it came on suddenly or gradually, in one eye or both, with or without pain, and whether it improves with blinking or looking through a pinhole separates refractive and surface problems from retinal, optic nerve and brain causes. Sudden loss of vision is itself a red flag, because most causes are serious.

**New causes:**
- Dry eye and conjunctivitis
- Glaucoma, including acute angle-closure
- Retinal vein or artery occlusion
- Optic neuritis
- Thyroid eye disease
- Medication effects (e.g., anticholinergic drugs)

**New red flags:**
- Halos around lights with a red, painful eye, headache and vomiting (possible acute angle-closure glaucoma)
- New floaters, flashes of light or a curtain over vision (possible retinal detachment)

**New references:**
- Merck Manual Professional - Acute Vision Loss: Brady CJ. Acute Vision Loss. Merck Manual Professional Version. Rahway (NJ): Merck & Co.; reviewed Oct 2025. https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/eye-disorders/symptoms-of-ophthalmic-disorders/acute-vision-loss

**New disease links:**
- graves_disease (3/10, x0.5)

## Breathlessness lying flat (orthopnea)  (`sym_orthopnea`)

**Added to the description:** Breathlessness that wakes the patient one to two hours after falling asleep and is relieved by sitting up (paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea) carries the same meaning. Orthopnea also occurs with pericardial effusion and with weakness of both diaphragms, where breathing worsens immediately on lying flat.

**New causes:**
- Pericardial effusion or tamponade
- Fluid overload in kidney disease or acute glomerulonephritis
- Rheumatic heart disease (mitral valve damage)
- Obesity

**New red flags:**
- Rapid weight gain with worsening leg or abdominal swelling

**New references:**
